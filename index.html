        Lesson

        The same chart, drawn two ways. In the North Indian diamond the houses keep their places, the first house always the top centre, and the signs move around them, so the eye reads bhavas first. In the South Indian square the signs keep their places, Mesha always the second cell of the top row, and the lagna is marked wherever it falls, so the eye reads rashis first.

        Nothing computed changes. The same grahas sit in the same signs and the same houses either way, and every table on the page reads the same. Pick whichever you learned to read: a chart in the other style takes a moment longer to parse and tells you nothing different.

        Which clock the recorded birth time was read off. Zone time is the answer for almost every chart: it is what a clock on the wall and a birth certificate give, and the timezone database applies the rules that were actually in force on the day, so a birth before standard time reached a country is already handled rather than assumed away.

        What it cannot handle is where in that country the birth was. For the years before standard time, the database keeps one local mean time for the whole zone, the one its reference city kept, which is out by up to an hour at the far end of a wide zone and an hour moves the ascendant fifteen degrees. Local mean time here means the birthplace’s own meridian, four minutes of time to the degree. It is the right reading for a birth recorded by the Sun where it happened, and the wrong one for any clock time.

        Where the sidereal zodiac begins, and so where every longitude on the page is measured from. There is no computing the right answer: the schools differ by more than a degree and a half, which moves a graha into another sign in about one reading in eleven and into another nakshatra or pada in four out of ten. Lahiri, or Chitrapaksha, is the one most Indian practice uses, and the default here.

        The nodes are where the Moon’s orbit crosses the ecliptic, and there is no single answer to where that is. The mean node regresses smoothly, a little over three arcminutes a day, every day. The true node is where the orbit actually crosses at that instant: it oscillates around the mean by up to about 1.9°, on a cycle of some 132 days, and it goes forward about a quarter of the time. The two part by about a degree on average, 1.9° at most.

        The practical ground for the mean node is that it is what Indian panchangs and the Lahiri ephemeris publish for Rahu and Ketu, so a chart read against one of those will agree. The true node is the default here because it is where the orbit actually crosses at the moment asked about.

        What that moves, measured over 4,800 charts: a chara karaka is reassigned in 21% of them, the nodes taking part in that reckoning; Rahu and Ketu fall in another nakshatra in 7%, another sign in 3% and another house in 3%; whether some graha is hemmed by benefics or by malefics changes in 2%, the nodes counting among the malefics that hem; and the list of yogas found changes in 1%.

        What it does not move: any Shadbala figure, including drik bala and the ranking. The six balas are reckoned for the seven grahas, and a node neither takes a figure nor casts an aspect that counts toward one. Nor the Vimshottari dasha, which runs off the Moon.

        A graha is combust when it stands too close to the Sun to be seen. The question a division raises is which pair of longitudes that distance is measured between.

        This site measures inside whichever division is drawn, so that every mark on a chart is a fact about that chart. A graha marked burnt beside a Sun twelve signs away reads as a fault in the page.

        That is a choice against the texts, and worth knowing as one. Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra ch.6: “The divisions of a combust planet, defeated planet, weak planet and a planet in bad Avasthas like Sayana be all ignored to be auspicious.” A combust graha has divisions; the divisions do not each have a combustion. Phaladeepika and Jataka Parijata put combustion at the foot of the dignity ladder, beside depression, and list vargottama apart from it.

        The arithmetic pulls the same way. A varga longitude is a rashi position stretched nine or ten times and wrapped, so two of them fall near each other about as often as two unrelated numbers would. Over 720 charts every graha comes out combust in 5 to 9 per cent of divisions whatever its orb or its orbit, and Mercury, really burnt in 42 per cent of charts and never much further than 28 degrees from the Sun, drops to 5. The second setting is the reading the texts ask for, and the card names the frame either way.

        How two grahas regard each other is graded twice over. The natural relation is a fixed table and never changes from one chart to the next. The temporal relation, tatkalika, is nothing but where the two happen to sit: Raman’s Graha and Bhava Balas, section 25, “as a result of the positions of planets at the birth time each stands at a certain relation with the other”. A graha in the 2nd, 3rd, 4th, 10th, 11th or 12th from another is its temporal friend; the rest, the 1st included, are enemies. The two halves added give the five-fold relation the vimsopaka grid is scored on.

        The question a division raises is which chart to count those houses in. Counting in the division keeps the whole relation inside one chart, since the sign being judged and the lord being judged against both come from there already. Counting in the rashi answers half the question in one chart and half in another.

        That second reading is the one with a text behind it. Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra gives the rule at verse 56 and then closes it “(This applies to a given Janm Kundali)”, the birth chart, and Santhanam adds the same to the saptavargaja verses, that compound relationships be seen in the Rashi chart only. No classical text asks for the divisional reading.

        It is the default here all the same, because a divisional chart is now read as a chart in its own right: its own houses, its own lords, its own relations. On that reading a relation half counted somewhere else is not an answer about the chart in front of you. That is modern practice rather than an authority, and worth knowing as one before leaving the setting alone.

        The cost of that default is measurable. Raman’s worked charts in Graha and Bhava Balas, Examples 1, 3 and 9, are built the rashi way, and read from the vargas they miss his own printed saptavargaja totals for six of the seven grahas in Example 9. Set this to the rashi and all three reconcile cell by cell. D1 is identical either way, a division of one being the rashi.

        The hora has only two signs, Cancer and Leo, so every lord in it is the Moon or the Sun. Read as the other divisions are read, the whole division collapses into one question about two grahas, and most of the ladder is unreachable: own sign by the luminaries alone, exaltation by Jupiter alone, debilitation by Mars alone.

        Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra asks a different question of it. Chapter 7: “Guru, Surya and Mangal give (pronounced) effects in the Hora of Surya. Candr, Sukr and Sani do so, when in Candr’s Horas; Budh is effective in both the Horas. In the case of an even Rasi the Hora of Candr will be powerful in effects, while Surya’s Hora in an odd Rasi will be so.” Both halves count here. A graha in the hora its own group favours takes the top rung where that hora is also the powerful one for the sign it came from, and the rung below where it is not. A graha outside its group takes the middle either way, and Mercury, effective in both horas, takes the top.

        That is a judgement about effects rather than about dignity, so it answers for the whole cell: the Sun is not reported as owning the Leo hora he merely gives pronounced effects in. The other reading is kept for reconciling against a table built the ordinary way, and shadbala keeps it regardless, because Raman scores his own Sun’s hora as Swavarga in Example 9 of Graha and Bhava Balas.

        Raman states a floor and most treatments drop it: “It should not be taken for granted that irrespective of the distance between the Sun and Mercury, Budha-Aditya Yoga would be present. On the contrary, Mercury should not be within 10 degrees of the Sun.” He gives no reason, and the obvious one does not hold: his own Hindu Predictive Astrology puts Mercury’s orb of combustion at 14° direct and 12° retrograde, so the floor cannot be combustion.

        K. N. Rao applies no floor. In Advance Techniques of Astrology Prediction he reads a chart - October 7 1964, 21:30 IST, Delhi - as “Mercury in fifth with the Sun forming Budhaditya yoga and in exaltation aspected by Jupiter” and counts it toward the promise of education. His own printed longitudes are Sun 21°03′ and Mercury 14°57′: six degrees apart, and combust under every orb any of these authors gives. The same book treats combust planets as adverse, so this is not an oversight. For Rao combustion is a discount on the yoga rather than a gate in front of it: whether the combination formed and how much it can deliver are two questions, and burning Mercury answers the second. That reading is applied on either setting, because it is a claim about what combustion does and not about where the boundary sits - so a combust Mercury that clears the floor is reported as a yoga formed and discounted, not as a contradiction.

        Raman’s reading is the default, on two grounds. He states a rule where Rao only declines to use one, and Mercury is never more than 28° from the Sun, so without a floor the yoga is claimed for every chart that has the two in one sign - 52 per cent of charts against 28. A finding half the world has tells you little about any of them.

        Parashara gives Mercury one qualifier - “Mercury, however, is a malefic if he joins a malefic” - and the authorities keep it. Raman, footnoting his drik bala table: “Mercury is a malefic as he is very closely associated with Sun or combusted.” K. N. Rao calls Mercury a malefic in a worked chart “as he is associated with two malefics Sun and Ketu”, and says combust planets “become evil”.

        The other reading is Parashara’s own opening list read without the sentence that follows it, which names Mercury a benefic beside the Moon, Jupiter and Venus; Rao gives the list the same way, “Jupiter, Venus, Mercury and Moon”. Neither defends it as the whole rule. It reaches paksha bala, drik bala, the kartari marks and every yoga that asks whether a graha is benefic.

        Nata-unnata measures how far the birth fell from noon or from midnight. Uttara Kalamrita gives it plainly: “Moon, Mars and Saturn are strong at midnight and powerless at noon. Ravi, Shukra and Guru are strong at noon, and powerless at midnight. Budha is always powerful.” The elapsed time divided by twelve is the figure.

        Which noon is the choice. The same text allows the clock: “It is enough if we take 12.00 hours as noon and zero hours as midnight.” Raman asks for the sundial by name: “The Hindus consider the apparent noon … it must be converted into the apparent time by applying equation of time.” They part because a timezone is an administrative band, and because a clock keeps an even hour where the Sun does not. Four virupas is typical, sixteen at the far west of a wide zone.

        Use apparent time, the default. Noon here means the Sun on the meridian, so it is what the measure is made of rather than a refinement of it. A birth time recorded in local mean time, as Indian times were before 1906, is a separate question: that is the time standard, in Chart settings.

        Saptavargaja bala scores a graha in each of seven divisions by how it stands with the lord of the sign it lands in. Seven rungs, from its own moolatrikona down to a great enemy’s sign, and the texts part below the second.

        Santhanam’s translation of Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra ch.27 and Saravali both give 45, 30, 20, 15, 10, 4 and 2. That is the default.

        Raman’s section 30 of Graha and Bhava Balas halves at every step below an own sign instead, giving 22.5, 7.5, 3.75 and 1.875 for four of the rungs. Uttara Kalamrita prints the same table at slokas 3.5-5 and Sripatipaddhati works an example in the same fractions, three-eighths of a rupa for a great friend’s varga, so it is better attested than Raman’s name alone suggests.

        Neither is a mistake, and the choice decides which published figures a reader can check against. The K. N. Rao textbook works its examples on the halving ladder, so set that before checking a chart against that book. It moves a total by about 5 shashtiamsas and turns about one verdict in forty.

        Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra ch.7 grades the hora by which grahas it favours and then by whether it is the powerful half of the sign. Six of the seven are covered plainly. The seventh is not, and the clause about it is “Budh is effective in both the Horas”.

        The default takes that wording as it stands. The six “give pronounced effects” in their own hora where Mercury “is effective” in both: a weaker claim, not a stronger one, so Mercury reaches the ordinary grade in either hora and never the pronounced one, and neither half of the sign makes any difference to it.

        Santhanam’s note on the same verse says the opposite, “Mercury is powerful in any Hora whether an odd sign or otherwise”, which is the fourth option. The second is a reading fitted to observation rather than taken from an authority: no classical text asks for it, and it is what two worked hora rows checked here want. The third treats Mercury like the other six, so it loses a rung in the weaker half.

        Worth knowing before leaving the default alone: the reference charts used here do not agree. Two hora rows want the second option and a vimsopaka column wants the first, and no reading of the clause satisfies all three.

        It reaches the vimsopaka grid and saptavargaja bala alike, and it can only ever move Mercury, and only in the hora.

        Paksha bala measures the fortnight rather than the graha. One figure goes to all the benefics and what is left of sixty to all the malefics. Brihat Jataka 21 puts it plainly: “The malefic and benefic planets have strength (Pakshabala) in the dark and bright halves of the month respectively.” Phaladeepika IV.1 says the same.

        So the only question is which group the Moon joins, and the texts answer it: she takes her turn in them, benefic while waxing and malefic while waning. Uttara Kalamrita gives the boundary as the eighth day of the bright half to the eighth of the dark, which is the reading used here, and doubles her figure. A thin Moon therefore scores high, having joined the malefics and taken the complement.

        The other option reads her always a benefic, so her figure follows her brightness alone. No classical text asks for it. The choice moves her paksha bala by up to sixty and changes nothing else, though it shows up twice, her cheshta bala being her paksha bala.

        The Moon’s paksha bala is doubled before it is counted. Uttara Kalamrita gives it in the verse itself: “The paksha bala of the Moon is to be doubled.” It attaches no condition, so it holds whichever group she is counted in, and the doubling is not disputed.

        Showing the row undoubled follows no authority. It makes a row-by-row comparison easier, since otherwise the row differs by a factor rather than by a figure. Only the display changes: Kala bala and every total still count the doubled value, so a halved cell will not appear to add up. Hover it and it names the figure the total is using.

        Hora bala gives sixty virupas to the lord of the planetary hour of birth, and how long that hour is turns out to be disputed after all.

        Parashara asks for equal hours, at chapter 27 of the Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra: “Each day from sunrise to sunrise is divided into 24 equal parts of one hour or 2.5 Ghatika.” The other scheme splits the daylight into twelve and the night into twelve, so an hour is long in summer and short in winter.

        The textbook by K. N. Rao’s students takes the second, dividing the day-length by twelve and the night-length by twelve, and works all three of its charts that way. That is the default here. It is the older planetary-hour scheme and the one the sequence of lords comes from, which runs in Chaldean order from the weekday lord and starts a night birth at the fifth from it.

        This is the most consequential of the small settings. The sixty is all or nothing, so the two schemes can hand it to different grahas and move a total by a whole rupa where the other choices here move it by a fraction of one.

        Ayana bala scales the declination against a maximum and divides by twice it. Both figures are sourced. Santhanam’s note to chapter 27 verses 15-17 of the Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra gives the formula as “(23°27′ + Kranti) × 1.2793”, where 23°27′ is 23.45 and 1.2793 is 60/46.9 exactly. Raman uses 24 and 48 instead.

        Each constant brings its own obliquity, because the two are one choice and not two. The scale only spans nought to sixty if the declination can reach the maximum: 23°27′ is near enough today’s obliquity, so Parashara’s pairs with the true declination, while Raman’s 24 pairs with a declination reckoned at 24. That is how the textbook by K. N. Rao’s students works it, reading the declination off a six-part table of the bhuja which is the exact declination for an obliquity of 24. Either way the measure touches both ends and leaves neither.

        Raman’s is the default, that textbook working all three of its charts with it, and the two part by at most 0.70 virupas on a graha and twice that on the Sun, whose figure is doubled. That is about half a shashtiamsa on a total and a strong-or-weak verdict in one reading in a thousand.

        A declination read off the sayana longitude alone, or the true declination with the graha’s latitude in it. Santhanam’s note to chapter 27 of the Brihat Parashara Hora Shastra sends the reader to an ephemeris for the second: “Krantis (or declinations) can be ascertained from standard modern ephemeris.” Raman derives the first from the bhuja of the sayana longitude, and a longitude carries no latitude.

        It is worth up to 2.5 virupas on a planet and 6.6 on the Moon, who swings furthest from the ecliptic: about two shashtiamsas on a total, and it reorders which graha is strongest in a little under a quarter of charts.

        Without latitude is the default, and the formula is the reason. A declination carrying latitude passes the obliquity - the Moon reaches 28 degrees - so the scale runs past its own sixty and below nought, and a negative strength is not a reading.

        The Sun’s ayana bala is doubled before it is counted. Parashara: “The Sun’s Ayana Bala is again multiplied by 2 whereas for others the product arrived in Virupas are considered as it is.” Raman gives it in one line in Graha and Bhava Balas, section 75: “And double the Ayanabala in the case of the Sun.” This one is not disputed either.

        Showing the row undoubled follows no authority, and is offered for the same reason as the Moon’s: a row that differs by a factor is hard to read against another table. Only the display changes. The doubled value is what Kala bala and every total count, and a halved cell names that figure on hover.

        Parashara gives both, in consecutive verses. At 21-23 he names eight motions and allots them 60, 30, 15, 30, 15, 7.5, 45 and 30; at 24-25 he gives the arithmetic instead, “Add together the mean and true longitudes ... Reduce this sum from the Seeghrocha ... divided by 3 which will denote the motional strength.” Raman follows the second, and so does this page by default, that being the verse that states a computation.

        The eight are taken from Sripatipaddhati rather than from those verses, Mantreswara sending the reader there for the computation at Phaladeepika IV.24 and Raman crediting the same author for the kendra. It has the same eight figures in the same order, and shows why the list reads so oddly in translation: the fourth of them, Samagama, is not a speed but conjunction with the Moon. With a non-speed in that slot the speed names shift by one, which is how “Sama”, the ordinary middling motion, comes to hold the smallest figure in the set.

        Sripati also gives the boundary, which is the mean motion itself: Manda and Mandatara fall below it, Seeghra and Seeghratara above, and each pair is split by whether the speed is still rising or already falling. Two things here are not his. How near to nothing a motion must be to count as Vikala, which is set at a twentieth of mean; and reading Samagama as sharing a sign with the Moon, the text saying only conjunction.

        The two measures correlate at 0.25, so this is a different quantity rather than a rounding of the other.

        The texts define one quantity and Sripatipaddhati names it: the cheshta kendra “is identical with the Seeghrakendra”, the arc of the seeghra correction in Indian planetary theory. Reaching it, its translator adds, “is very elaborate and involves a good knowledge of the laws of planetary motion, their Manda and Seeghra phalas”.

        So both he and Raman give a shortcut instead, and Raman calls his “a simple method” at section 83 of Graha and Bhava Balas in as many words: the seeghrocha less half the sum of the mean and true longitudes, which stands in for the manda-corrected place the real arc is measured from. That is the second option, and it is what every worked example in Raman is computed with.

        Sripati prints accurate kendras for his own 1853 example, taken from Ketakar’s tables, and they separate the two cleanly. The seeghra kendra reproduces them to 0.34 of a virupa; the shortcut averages 3.41 and misses Budha by eight, his manda correction being the largest. Against Raman’s Example 51 it is the other way about, 0.04 against 1.74, each method answering to the book that uses it. The default is the arc the texts define.

        The chesta kendra is the gap between a graha’s true longitude and a mean one. A true longitude is an observation; a mean longitude is a fiction, the place a graha would hold if it moved uniformly, and a fiction belongs to whichever model invented it. Pairing a modern mean with a modern true is self-consistent without being what any text computes.

        Raman pairs an accurate ephemeris with the Surya Siddhanta tables, at sections 96 to 103 of Graha and Bhava Balas. On the averaged kendra that pairing reproduces his Example 51 to 0.02 virupas, where modern means miss Budha by 4.29. The rates here are the Surya Siddhanta’s own, each graha’s revolutions in a Mahayuga over its 1,577,917,828 civil days.

        Be clear what the choice is. The classical tables are better at reproducing what the books print, and are not a better description of the sky: measured against the seeghra kendra the shortcut stands in for, modern means come out at 3.27 virupas and classical at 3.40, so the shortcut’s own error is twenty-five times the gap between them.

        On the default this setting does nothing. The seeghra kendra takes both its places off the same ephemeris, so there is no seam to choose; the question only arises once the averaged shortcut above is selected.

        Neither luminary retrogrades, so neither has an arc of retrogression to measure. Parashara answers twice, once in each chapter, and the two answers are a pair.

        At 27.18, among the shadbalas: “The Sun’s Cheshta Bala will correspond to his Ayana Bala. The Moon’s Paksha Bala will itself be her Cheshta Bala.” At 28.3-4, opening Ishta and Kashta, each is given a kendra of its own instead: “Add 3 Rashis to Sayana Sun, which will be the Cheshta Kendra for the Sun. The sidereal longitude of the Sun should be deducted from the Moon to get the Moon’s Cheshta Kendra.” Reduced past six signs and divided by three, as every cheshta kendra is.

        The kendras are the default, because that is the chapter the figure is for: it is not added to the shadbala sum. Raman works the Sun this way at Example 60 of Graha and Bhava Balas, reaching 22.66 where the ayana bala on that chart gives 18.95. For the Moon the two rules agree while she is read as a benefic, and are complements of sixty under the group reading.

        Raman does not add the luminaries’ cheshta bala to the total. Example 56 of Graha and Bhava Balas leaves both rows of his table empty, and the totals he prints are the sums without them, Chandra’s 389.80 to the hundredth. Section 136 of Graha and Bhava Balas says why the figure is wanted at all: it is “necessary to ascertain the Ishta and Kashta Phalas”, the subject of Parashara’s Ishta and Kashta chapter. So by default it is shown and not counted.

        Counting it is the other reading, since Parashara does give the two a cheshta bala at 27.18 and does list cheshta among the six at 27.24-25. Be careful pairing that with the borrowed rule above, though: the Sun’s ayana bala and the Moon’s paksha bala already sit inside kala bala, and doubled there, so counting the borrowed figure again puts it in at three times its base where the doubling asks for twice. That is worth about a rupa on each.

        Not a seventh share. Ishta is the good a graha is disposed to do in its dasha and kashta the harm, and both are read off uchcha bala and cheshta bala alone. This is what the luminaries’ cheshta bala is computed for.

        Sripatipaddhati IV.6: “Multiply the figures representing the two phalas. The square root of the resulting product expresses the good due to the Uchcha and the Cheshta balas of the planets. Subtract the two phalas separately from unity and multiply the remainders together. The square root of the product gives the evil.” Kesava states it the same way, and Raman’s sections 138-139 of Graha and Bhava Balas are that rule in English. It is the default.

        Parashara ch.28.6 divides a fixed sixty instead: ishta is half the sum of the two balas and kashta is sixty less it. Sastri, commenting on Sripatipaddhati IV.6, sets the two side by side and says Parashara “lays down a different rule”. They part where a graha is low in both shares, which the square roots let it be and the halving cannot.
