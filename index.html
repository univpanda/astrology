      Lesson

      Chart settings

      Where the sidereal zodiac begins, and so where every longitude on the page is measured from. There is no computing the right answer: the schools differ by more than a degree and a half, which moves a graha into another sign in about one reading in eleven and into another nakshatra or pada in four out of ten. Lahiri, or Chitrapaksha, is the default here, being the one most Indian practice uses: Charak calls his ephemeris “the best” and his tables “the most popular”, and de Fouw and Svoboda say “the majority of Indian jyotishis use” it. It is widely called the Indian government standard, which could not be confirmed here: the Calendar Reform Committee of 1952 under Meghnad Saha recommended the national civil calendar, and neither its published remit nor the Rashtriya Panchang’s own pages name an ayanamsa.

      The nodes are where the Moon’s orbit crosses the ecliptic, and there is no single answer to where that is. The mean node regresses smoothly, a little over three arcminutes a day, every day. The true node is where the orbit actually crosses at that instant: it oscillates around the mean by up to about 1.9°, on a cycle of some 132 days, and it goes forward about a quarter of the time. The two part by about a degree on average, 1.9° at most.

      What that moves, measured over 4,800 charts: a chara karaka is reassigned in 21% of them, the nodes taking part in that reckoning; Rahu and Ketu fall in another nakshatra in 7%, another sign in 3% and another house in 3%; a [P] or [S] mark moves on some graha in 2%, the nodes counting as malefics for the hemming; and the list of yogas found changes in 1%.

      What it does not move: any Shadbala figure, including drik bala and the ranking. The six balas are reckoned for the seven grahas, and a node neither takes a figure nor casts an aspect that counts toward one. Nor the Vimshottari dasha, which runs off the Moon.

      Paksha bala gives one figure to the benefics and what is left of sixty to the malefics. Whether the Moon takes her turn in those groups is genuinely disputed. Santhanam’s notes to chapter 27 have “The Moon in dark half is a malefic”, and then that her paksha bala is doubled “whether the Moon is in a group of benefices or otherwise” - which only needs saying if she can fall in either; Raman puts the same rule inside the paksha chapter at section 53. Against that, K. S. Charak lists her among the natural benefics for this bala without qualification, and Phaladeepika IV.5 reads the same way: “The Moon is strong and auspicious when she has her full Paksha bala”, which cannot hold under the group rule, where it is the dark Moon that carries the high figure.

      It shows up twice, because the Moon’s cheshta bala is her paksha bala. Only her own figure moves: the other six are untouched, and her benefic standing for drik bala and the yogas is read the ordinary way whichever is chosen here. The doubling is not in dispute - all three authorities apply it.

      Two figures in the table are doubled before they are counted. Parashara: “The Sun’s Ayana Bala is again multiplied by 2 whereas for others the product arrived in Virupas are considered as it is.” Raman says the same at section 75, and again at section 55 rule (c) for the Moon’s paksha bala. Charak directs the Moon’s to be doubled too. None of it is disputed.

      Some software prints these two rows undoubled, which follows no authority but does make a row-by-row comparison easier, since otherwise they differ by a factor rather than by a figure. This setting changes only what is shown. Kala bala and every total still count the doubled value, so a halved cell will not appear to add up: hover it and it names the figure the total is using.

      Nata-unnata measures how far the birth fell from midnight, and the question is whose midnight. Raman asks for the sundial by name at section 48: “Midday of any place is the local noon when the Sun passes over its meridian. The Hindus consider the apparent noon … if birth time is marked in local mean time, it must be converted into the apparent time by applying equation of time.”

      That is two corrections away from the clock, and both are commonly skipped. A timezone is an administrative band, so a place can sit an hour or more from the meridian it keeps time by; and even on that meridian a clock keeps mean time, which the equation of time parts from the Sun by up to sixteen minutes either way. At New York the two together are worth about 4.6 virupas, at Delhi 3, and at the western edge of a wide zone as much as 14.

      No authority asks for zone time. It is offered because widely used software computes it that way, and a row differing for that reason is easy to mistake for a difference of method.

      Hora bala gives sixty virupas to the lord of the planetary hour of birth, and how long that hour is turns out not to be disputed. Raman, section 69: “A hora is equal to 1/24th part of a day.” Santhanam, on chapter 27: “Each day from sunrise to sunrise is divided into 24 equal parts of one hour or 2.5 Ghatika.” Charak, citing Aryabhata: “There are 24 Horas in a day, each Hora being (approximately!) equivalent to an hour.”

      The alternative splits the daylight into twelve and the night into twelve, so an hour is long in summer and short in winter. It is the older planetary-hour scheme and some software uses it, but no source consulted here asks for it in this bala. It is offered because the two can hand the sixty to different grahas - on one chart checked they gave Mars and Jupiter - and that is worth being able to recognise rather than puzzle over.

      Ayana bala scales a graha’s declination, and there are two complete methods rather than one. Raman derives the kranti at section 73 from the bhuja of the sayana longitude, through a table of six fifteen-degree steps, and a longitude carries no latitude; his Example 32 works all seven grahas that way, the Moon included. That pairs with his divisor of 48, because 24 degrees is what a declination read off a longitude reaches.

      Parashara’s is the other whole method. Santhanam’s note to chapter 27 sends the reader straight to a modern ephemeris - “Krantis (or declinations) can be ascertained from standard modern ephemeris” - which gives the true declination, ecliptic latitude and all.

      Worth up to 2.5 virupas on a planet and 6.6 on the Moon, who swings furthest from the ecliptic. Drik Panchang computes the true declination and reproduces exactly on the second setting.

      Mantreswara states it symmetrically. Phaladeepika ch.6 shloka 14: “If, at a day-birth in the case of a male, the Sun, the Moon and the Lagna are in odd signs, the Mahabhagya Yoga is formed. The same Yoga in the case of females will arise when the birth is at night and the Sun, the Moon and the Lagna are posited in even signs.”

      Raman keeps the night for women and drops the day for men: “In the case of a man, the Sun, the Moon and the Lagna should be in odd signs. In case of women, when the birth is during night, the Sun, the Moon and Lagna must be in even signs.” Two scans of the 1947 edition read alike, so it is not a slip of the printing, and his worked chart settles that he means it: combination 25 takes a man born at 8-15 p.m. - night - and reads “all odd signs, consequently Mahabhagya Yoga is fully present.”

      Phaladeepika is the default. He states the fuller rule and states it evenly, where Raman's two halves do not match each other; and the day test halves how often the reading is claimed for a man, from 12.5 per cent of charts to 6.2.

      Raman states a floor and most treatments drop it: “It should not be taken for granted that irrespective of the distance between the Sun and Mercury, Budha-Aditya Yoga would be present. On the contrary, Mercury should not be within 10 degrees of the Sun.” He gives no reason, and the obvious one does not hold: his own Hindu Predictive Astrology puts Mercury’s orb of combustion at 14° direct and 12° retrograde, so the floor cannot be combustion.

      K. N. Rao applies no floor. In Advance Techniques of Astrology Prediction he reads a chart - October 7 1964, 21:30 IST, Delhi - as “Mercury in fifth with the Sun forming Budhaditya yoga and in exaltation aspected by Jupiter” and counts it toward the promise of education. His own printed longitudes are Sun 21°03′ and Mercury 14°57′: six degrees apart, and combust under every orb any of these authors gives. The same book treats combust planets as adverse, so this is not an oversight. For Rao combustion is a discount on the yoga rather than a gate in front of it: whether the combination formed and how much it can deliver are two questions, and burning Mercury answers the second. That reading is applied on either setting, because it is a claim about what combustion does and not about where the boundary sits - so a combust Mercury that clears the floor is reported as a yoga formed and discounted, not as a contradiction.

      Raman’s reading is the default, on two grounds. He states a rule where Rao only declines to use one, and Mercury is never more than 28° from the Sun, so without a floor the yoga is claimed for every chart that has the two in one sign - 52 per cent of charts against 28. A finding half the world has tells you little about any of them.

      Parashara gives Mercury one qualifier - “Mercury, however, is a malefic if he joins a malefic” - and the authorities keep it. Raman, footnoting his drik bala table: “Mercury is a malefic as he is very closely associated with Sun or combusted.” Charak: “well-associated Mercury” is a natural benefic and “afflicted Mercury” a natural malefic, since Mercury “behaves as a benefic under benefic influence and as a malefic under malefic influence.” K. N. Rao calls Mercury a malefic in a worked chart “as he is associated with two malefics Sun and Ketu”, and says combust planets “become evil”.

      The other reading is the same authors’ opening list without the sentence that follows it - Charak’s “Natural benefics: Moon, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus”, Rao’s “Jupiter, Venus, Mercury and Moon”. None of them defends it as the whole rule, but software computes it, and Drik Panchang reads Mercury benefic on a chart where he sits ten degrees from the Sun in the same sign. It reaches paksha bala, drik bala, the kartari marks and every yoga that asks whether a graha is benefic.

      Parashara gives both, in consecutive verses. At 21-23 he names eight motions and allots them 60, 30, 15, 30, 15, 7.5, 45 and 30; at 24-25 he gives the arithmetic - “Add together the mean and true longitudes ... Reduce this sum from the Seeghrocha ... divided by 3 which will denote the motional strength.” Raman follows the second, Charak gives only the first. The default here is the arithmetic, since that is the verse that states a computation.

      Be aware of what the second option costs. Vakra is retrograde and Anuvakra is retrogression across a sign boundary, and the ephemeris settles both outright. The other six motions are bands on a speed, and neither Parashara nor Charak says where stationary ends, where slow becomes slower, or where fast becomes very fast. So all five boundaries dividing them are this site’s choice and not anybody’s text: 0.05, 0.40, 0.75, 1.25 and 1.75 of a graha’s mean daily motion, the inner two measured against the Sun’s.

      The two measures correlate at only 0.32, so this is a different quantity rather than a rounding of the other. Checked against two outside calculators on three charts, the eight-motion table is further from both than the kendra is.
